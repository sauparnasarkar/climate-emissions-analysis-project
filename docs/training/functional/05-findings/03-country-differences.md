# Findings: Country Differences

> Part of the [Functional Series](../README.md). Global totals hide very different national
> stories. This document summarises what the platform's data show for the ten featured countries.
> The comparisons use gross emissions excluding land use, so they indicate direction and distance,
> not whether a pledge will be met; the scope limits are set out in
> [Country NDCs §3](../04-paris-agreement/03-country-ndcs.md).
>
> **As of:** platform data to 2024 (PRIMAP-hist total GHG, whose country values are rounded to
> three significant figures; OWID CO₂); UNEP *Emissions Gap Report 2025* for pledges and
> assessments.

## Finding 10. Emissions have peaked in some featured countries and are still rising in others

Change in total greenhouse gas emissions (excluding land use) from **2019** to 2024, the base
year of the IPCC pathways, with the year of the series' peak:

| Country | Peak of the series | 2024 vs 2019 | Group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany | 1979 | about −19% | Peaked long ago; falling |
| United Kingdom | 1979 | about −16% | Peaked long ago; falling |
| Japan | around 2007–2013 | about −12% | Peaked; falling |
| Australia | 2018 | about −7% | Recently peaked; falling slowly |
| United States | 2007 | about −6% | Peaked; falling slowly |
| Russia | 1988 | about +4% | Well below its peak; rising again since 2019 |
| South Africa | 2009 | about +4% | Near a plateau |
| Brazil | 2023 | about +8% | At or near a record |
| China | 2024 | about +13% | At a record high |
| India | 2024 | about +16% | At a record high |

**Evidence.** Platform series, [Country NDCs §3](../04-paris-agreement/03-country-ndcs.md);
[Data Sources](../01-data-sources/01-emissions-inventories.md).

**Confidence.** High for direction (large differences against rounding of three significant
figures). The peak years for Japan, Brazil and the United States are close ties and should not be
read to the year.

**Does not say.** That the falling countries are on track for their targets (Finding 11), or
that the rising ones are doing worse *per person* or *per unit of output*. Emissions per capita
and per unit of GDP are separate questions the platform shows on other pages.

## Finding 11. Falling emissions are not the same as meeting a target

**Finding.** Three featured countries are measured against 1990 (Germany about −48%, the UK
about −52%, Russia about −33%, on gross figures), and they are in different positions against
their targets:

- **Russia** is already past its 2030 pledge on a gross basis, and UNEP projects it about 25%
  below its target level.
- **The UK** is at −52% against a pledge of at least −68% by 2030, and UNEP assesses it as less
  likely to meet it on current policies (official UK projections agree).
- **Germany** shares the EU's collective NDC (−55% by 2030); on the platform's BAU it reaches
  about −52% by 2030.
- **Japan** (about −25% against 2013, a pledge of −46%) and **Australia** (a small gross change
  against a pledge of −43%) are described by UNEP as "very close" to meeting their 2030 targets on current policies. Gross
  figures can overstate the distance where the pledges are net of land removals.

**Evidence.** [Country NDCs §2–3](../04-paris-agreement/03-country-ndcs.md).

**Confidence.** Moderate: the direction of each country's gap is clear; the size depends on scope
(net versus gross, base year).

**Does not say.** Anything about which countries will meet their targets. UNEP's policy-based
assessment is the right source for that.

## Finding 12. Growth is concentrated in China and India, and the trend model is least reliable for China

**Finding.** China and India show the largest growth since 2019 (about +13% and +16% in total
greenhouse gases; China's roughly 1.8 GtCO₂e is the largest in absolute terms) and both are at
record highs in 2024. China is also where the
platform's statistical forecast most plausibly **overstates** future emissions: its BAU rises
about 10% by 2030, whereas UNEP's policy-based studies show a peak around 2025 followed by a
fall of 0.3–1.4 GtCO₂e by 2030, driven by renewable generation growing faster than power demand.

**Evidence.** [Country NDCs §2–3](../04-paris-agreement/03-country-ndcs.md);
[Forecasting §4](../02-methodology/02-forecasting.md).

**Confidence.** Moderate. The statistical model is a continuation of 1990–2024 behaviour and,
by design, cannot see policy or technology change. For a country in structural transition, that
is the largest single limit on the platform's forecasts.

**Does not say.** That China's emissions have peaked. In the platform's data, 2024 is the
highest year yet.

## Finding 13. Pledges measure different things, so they cannot be ranked on one scale

**Finding.** The ten featured countries' pledges take different forms: China's combines a peak
before 2030 with a carbon-intensity target, India's is an emissions-intensity target, South
Africa's is an absolute range, and the rest are percentage reductions from base years running
from 1990 (the EU, UK and Russia) to 2013 (Japan). Several are stated net of land removals
(Brazil and the EU, for example).

**Evidence.** [Country NDCs §1](../04-paris-agreement/03-country-ndcs.md).

**Confidence.** High for the description; the consequences are summarised in
[Caveats](04-caveats-and-reading-guide.md).

**Does not say.** That any pledge is more or less ambitious than another. That needs a common
metric, such as per-capita emissions or an equity-based fair share, which the platform does not
compute.
